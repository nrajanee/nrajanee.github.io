I've been feeling a lot of disgust and sadness with the phone addiction I've been
noticing in society. Many people know most of what I've written below, but they
don't seem to have internalized the extent of the consequences at an individual
or societal level.

I started to observe this more after my mother got annoyed that I didn't respond
to some guy promptly, and my friend got a bit upset because I take days to respond
to texts. My friend understood that I have certain boundaries and changed his
expectations. But I also wonder why those expectations were there in the first
place. Why does society think it's normal to respond to texts immediately, or even
within the same day? Weren't calls meant for time-sensitive responses?

Tech companies designed texts and notifications to feel like slot machines. I
don't want to be a slave to a slot machine. To rebel against this well-designed
slavery, I have boundaries with my phone. My phone is usually on focus mode and I
don't look at it while working. I only receive calls on my laptop. Outside of work
too, I try to stay off my phone. I also make sure not to look at my phone for 30
minutes before sleeping and after waking up.

When I do see my phone and notice calls and texts, I'm more compelled to call
someone back than to text — especially if it's not time-sensitive or a yes-or-no
kind of question. A call is a one-time interaction; it doesn't create dopamine
loops and it's more natural. A text is mono-sensory, doesn't feel complete, leaves
you in anticipation, and it can go back and forth, back and forth, creating
dopamine spikes and frying my brain. Frying all our brains.

Also, isn't it draining to constantly create space for other people's stories and
emotions? If it's not, do you think you're focused on creating stories for
yourself? Or what am I missing? I genuinely do appreciate getting an occasional
text from a friend, but I want to be intentional about responding. I want to make
sure I have space when I respond to them. And I always want to prioritize
real-life, deep interactions — not just with humans, but with my work, books,
nature, dance, cinema, music, and with myself. For me a text is like a letter
delivered by a pigeon, so I think it's normal to take a while to respond.

I feel the same way about doom scrolling and the brain rot it causes. I'm posting
this on Instagram. I know. My excuse is that I typically post every two weeks, and
I limit Instagram use in between (max 5–10 minutes every few days, to stalk a
friend I haven't spoken to in a while). I physically cannot doom scroll because
I'm extremely sensitive to noise and lots of visual stimulation — my heart rate
increases and I get very anxious. How are people using doom scrolling to get away
from their anxiety and boredom? Instagram used to be about spending a few minutes
seeing what your friends were up to, and that's it, right? Now the creators keep
creating and everyone else keeps consuming. And to what end?

AI is genuinely getting smarter every day, and the only thing that will
differentiate us from AI is our unique perspectives and experiences. By consuming
so much content, aren't we diluting those perspectives? By constantly responding
to texts, aren't we creating more space for others and less space for our own
real-life experiences? Why are the present, real-life interactions — where all
five senses are being used — not enough? Why do people prefer being bombarded and
controlled by this device?

This feels like a sickness. Please tell me what I'm missing. It really depresses me
to see people on the subway looking at their phone. To see people in the gym
looking at their phone. To see people at the park looking at their phone. It breaks
my heart that so many people are choosing to spend so much of their life on that
device. Look at your phone's screen time and the number of pickups — are you okay
with being enslaved by your phone? Why isn't everyone choosing to be more
disciplined and intentional about this? A full day without your phone should be
comfortable. Why is that so difficult for most people?
